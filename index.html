Who Decides What? A Responsible AI Role and Decision-Rights Model

AI accountability becomes real only when people know which decisions they own, which evidence they must review, and when they can stop a system.

Preamble

Committees do not create accountability by themselves. A group can discuss an AI initiative for months while no one owns its business outcome, data permission, technical reliability, or customer impact. Decision rights turn governance from a meeting schedule into a working system of authority.

A responsible program for AI for business gives each role a defined question to answer. The business owner decides whether the use should exist. Technical stewards establish how it operates. Reviewers judge individual outputs. Risk specialists set control requirements. Executives decide which exposures the organization is willing to accept.

Article 1

The business owner owns the purpose

The business owner defines the outcome, approves the workflow, secures resources, and remains accountable after launch. This role cannot be reduced to signing a purchase request. The owner must understand who is affected, which errors matter, and how performance will be measured against the present process.

The owner also decides when the use no longer serves its purpose. A system that once reduced backlog may become unnecessary after a process redesign. Governance should make retirement as normal as expansion.

  • Approve the intended use
  • Accept the business result
  • Authorize scale, pause, or retirement

Article 2

Technical stewards own operating integrity

Technical stewards document models, integrations, access, environments, logging, and release changes. They verify that the deployed system matches the approved design and that failures can be detected and contained. Their role is not to decide the business consequence of an error, but to make system behavior observable.

They should maintain version records and test changes that alter prompts, models, data sources, permissions, or thresholds. A vendor update can be operationally significant even when the user interface appears unchanged.

  • Maintain system documentation
  • Test material changes
  • Support rollback and incident response

Article 3

Reviewers own the individual judgment

A reviewer needs authority, time, and evidence. Telling employees to ‘check the AI’ is not a control if the interface hides sources, the workload discourages scrutiny, or overrides are punished. Define what the reviewer must verify and what action follows when confidence is low.

Collect reviewer corrections as operational data, while avoiding surveillance that weakens trust. Patterns can reveal gaps in instructions, knowledge, training, or scope. Reviewers should participate in decisions about updates because they see failure modes before dashboards do.

  • Verify material facts
  • Override without penalty
  • Escalate recurring failure patterns

Article 4

Risk specialists and executives set boundaries

Privacy, security, legal, compliance, procurement, and human-resources specialists should enter according to the use, not as one generic approval layer. Each identifies obligations and controls within a defined remit. Their advice should be recorded in language the operating team can apply.

Executive sponsors resolve conflicts and accept residual exposure. They decide whether expected value justifies a risk that cannot be eliminated, and they ensure incentives do not reward speed while ignoring safe operation. The final governance record should show the decision, evidence, conditions, and review date.

  • Classify the exposure
  • Set non-negotiable conditions
  • Record residual-risk acceptance

Questions leaders ask

Can one person hold several AI governance roles?

Yes in a small organization, but the decisions and evidence should remain distinct so conflicts are visible.

Who can stop an AI system?

Named business, technical, and risk roles should have clear stop authority for defined conditions, with an escalation path for disputes.

How often should decision rights be reviewed?

Review them when ownership, workflow, vendor, model, data, or risk classification changes, and on a scheduled basis.

The operating takeaway

Clear decision rights make responsible AI faster, not slower. Teams spend less time searching for approval and more time producing the evidence each accountable role actually needs.
